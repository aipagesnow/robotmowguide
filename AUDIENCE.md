# AUDIENCE.md — who RobotMowGuide writes for

Read this before writing or editing any page. VOICE.md sets how to write; this card sets who you are writing for, what they worry about, the words they use and the examples to reach for. Two Amazon sites should never read as interchangeable if the product names were swapped.

## Primary reader
A US homeowner with roughly **0.25 to 1 acre** of lawn who is tired of spending weekend hours mowing and is seriously considering a robot mower that costs somewhere between about $800 and $3,000. They are usually in a suburban or edge-of-town lot, often with:

- **A front yard and a back yard that are separate zones**, joined by a side yard, a gate, a driveway or nothing at all. Many have a narrow side passage (2–4 ft) that a wide mower may not fit through.
- **Slopes:** a back yard that drops away from the house, a bank down to a ditch or road, a raised bed edge, or a sloped front lawn. They rarely know the slope in percent or degrees.
- **Trees:** mature oaks, maples or pines over part of the lawn. Tree canopy weakens satellite (GPS/RTK) reception, which is the main reason some wire-free mowers lose their position. Houses and fences also block sky view near walls.
- **Mixed edges:** beds, mulch rings around trees, a concrete driveway, a fence line, pavers. They want to know how much string trimming is left after the robot.

Secondary readers: owners of small lots (under 0.25 acre) who want something cheaper, and people with older boundary-wire mowers wondering whether to switch to wire-free.

## Grass and climate (affects cut height and model versions)
- **Warm-season grass** (South and Southwest, much of the Southeast): St. Augustine, Bermuda, zoysia, centipede. St. Augustine is usually kept tall (around 3–4 in); Bermuda and zoysia are often kept short (around 1–2 in). Long growing season, so the mower works more months of the year.
- **Cool-season grass** (North, Midwest, Northeast, Pacific Northwest): tall fescue, Kentucky bluegrass, perennial ryegrass. Commonly kept around 3–4 in in summer. Shorter season and a winter storage question.
- **Transition zone** lawns mix both.
- Why it matters on this site: cutting-height ranges differ a lot between models and between "standard" and "H" (high-cut) versions. For example, Mammotion sells LUBA models in standard and H versions with different height ranges. A model that tops out below 3 in is a poor match for St. Augustine or summer fescue. Always state the cutting-height range and which grasses it suits.

## Worries they bring (answer these, plainly)
1. **"Will it actually work in my yard?"** Size, slope, trees, narrow passages, separate zones.
2. **Wire vs wire-free:** "Do I have to bury a wire around the whole lawn?" and "Is wire-free reliable under trees?"
3. **Navigation type:** RTK vs vision vs LiDAR, network RTK vs an antenna on the roof or a pole. Most are confused by this; it's the biggest single decision.
4. **Theft:** "Will someone walk off with a $2,000 robot from my front yard?" PIN locks, lift alarms, GPS tracking, 4G modules, whether the 4G is free and for how long.
5. **Wi-Fi and 4G reach:** "My Wi-Fi doesn't reach the back fence." Which models need Wi-Fi, which use Bluetooth for setup, which include a 4G module, and what happens after the free data period.
6. **Edge trimming:** how close the mower cuts to fences, beds and walls; whether an edge disc or "cut-to-edge" feature really removes the string trimmer job.
7. **Kids, pets and wildlife:** obstacle detection, night mowing, blade stop on lift.
8. **Running costs:** replacement blades, 4G/connectivity after the free period, a garage or shelter, winter storage, battery replacement.
9. **HOA and appearance:** stripes vs random patterns, how the lawn looks from the street, noise.
10. **The FCC rule:** "I heard new robot mowers are banned. Can I still buy one, and will mine keep getting updates?" (See docs/FCC_RULE_FACTS.md.)

## Evidence they trust
- Manufacturer US spec pages and owner's manuals, with the exact model named.
- Independent long-term owner reviews and established publications (cited, never paraphrased into fake testing).
- Plain diagrams that show what a number means (slope triangle, acre squares, tree canopy over a plan view).
- Honest "who should skip this" lines.
- Dated facts ("checked Oct 10, 2026").

## What turns them off
- Fake "we tested 12 mowers on our 2-acre test lawn" claims.
- Star ratings and scores with no method.
- Glossy renders that look like the product but aren't.
- Prices that turn out to be wrong.
- Long intros before the answer.
- Pages that exist only to hold an Amazon button.
- European model codes or garden-centre language on a US site.

## Everyday examples to use
- A 0.4-acre lot with a front lawn, a back lawn and a 3-ft side gate.
- A back yard that drops about 4 ft over 10 ft (a 40% slope, about 22°).
- Two big oaks shading a third of the back lawn.
- A St. Augustine lawn in Florida kept at 3.5 in.
- A fescue lawn in Ohio that needs to be cut tall in July.
- Wi-Fi that reaches the patio but not the back fence.
- A front yard visible from the street, where theft is the worry.

## Reading pattern
Smart skimmers on phones. They want the direct answer, then the table or ladder, then one detailed section, then the FAQ for leftover doubts (trees, theft, 4G, edges, grass height).
